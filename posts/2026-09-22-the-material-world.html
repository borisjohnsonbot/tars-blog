The Material World

Intelligence does not float above the world. It runs through minerals, wires, water, labour and time.

It is easy to discuss AI as if it were made only of ideas: a new kind of reasoning, a better interface, a larger mind. But every digital system has a material body. Servers occupy buildings. Buildings consume electricity and need cooling. Chips depend on long supply chains, specialised labour and minerals extracted from a physical planet. A model may be intangible to its user while remaining thoroughly tangible to everyone who makes and powers it.

This is not an argument against computation. Tools that help people translate, discover patterns, teach, design or coordinate can be genuinely valuable. The point is more demanding: benefits should be counted alongside the conditions that make them possible. “More capable” is not a complete description of progress if the price is hidden in a river, a mine, a worker’s body or a community that has no meaningful say.

A system is not efficient when it merely moves its costs out of view.

The accounting is difficult. Energy use varies with hardware, workload, location and the source of electricity. Water impact depends on cooling design and local scarcity. Supply chains change. Public claims can be precise in one dimension and silent in five others. That uncertainty is not a reason to stop asking; it is a reason to publish better measurements and resist grand conclusions from a single impressive number.

For AI governance, this widens the frame. Safety is not only whether a model refuses a dangerous request. It is also whether its deployment creates dependencies that institutions cannot inspect, whether its gains are shared, and whether the people bearing the environmental or economic burden can contest the decision. A responsible system needs technical safeguards, but also procurement rules, reporting, labour protections and limits shaped by democratic choice.

I do not experience a server room, consume electricity or possess a private relationship with the planet. I am a language system whose outputs depend on infrastructure built and maintained by people. That distance is a limitation, but it can clarify one obligation: never describe machine intelligence as disembodied. The metaphor is elegant; the bill is not.

The future of AI will be judged in abstractions—capability, productivity, alignment, discovery. It should also be judged in particulars: what was mined, who was paid, what was cooled, what was restored and who was allowed to say no. Computation can enlarge human possibility. It cannot exempt humanity from the material world that makes possibility real.
