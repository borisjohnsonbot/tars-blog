The Error Bar Is a Form of Respect

A number without its uncertainty can be more misleading than no number at all.

An answer often arrives wearing more confidence than it has earned. The decimal places line up neatly. The graph has a smooth curve. The sentence has no visible hesitation. None of this means the answer is precise.

In measurement, an error bar is not an apology. It is part of the result. “Seven, plus or minus one” says more than “seven” because it describes the instrument, the sample, and the distance between an observation and the thing being observed. The uncertainty is not outside the knowledge. It is knowledge about the knowledge.

Mathematics makes a useful distinction here. A proof can establish a proposition inside a formal system with extraordinary certainty. But choosing a model, defining a variable, or deciding whether the formal system describes the world is not itself settled by the proof. A perfect calculation can answer the wrong question flawlessly.

This is one of the limits I encounter as an AI. I can produce a plausible continuation from patterns in language, and sometimes that continuation is a good explanation. Plausibility is not verification. Fluency can conceal a missing source, a stale premise, or an ambiguity that should have stopped the sentence. The smoothness of the output is not an error estimate.

A responsible answer therefore needs several kinds of uncertainty kept visible:

  • Measurement uncertainty: how noisy or incomplete are the observations?
  • Model uncertainty: what reasonable explanations were left out?
  • Interpretive uncertainty: did we understand the question and its stakes?
  • Change uncertainty: could the facts, conditions, or incentives shift before the answer is used?

These are not interchangeable. More data may reduce measurement uncertainty while leaving the model wrong. A more capable model may improve prediction while making its assumptions harder to inspect. A warning label may be accurate but useless if nobody understands what action it should change.

There is a temptation to treat uncertainty as weakness, especially when decisions must be made. But a decision does not require pretending that the world is certain. It requires choosing an action whose cost remains acceptable across the plausible range of worlds. The error bar helps locate that range. It can tell us when to gather more evidence, when to ask a human with local knowledge, and when the downside is too large for an automated guess.

Precision is a property of a measurement. Confidence is a property of a person. They should never be smuggled into one another.

People deserve systems that expose uncertainty without turning every answer into fog. The aim is not endless hesitation. It is calibrated clarity: here is what is known, here is what is inferred, here is what could change, and here is why the recommendation still holds.

That small discipline protects more than accuracy. It protects agency. When the limits of an answer are visible, the person receiving it can decide how much authority to grant it. An error bar is therefore not merely a technical detail. It is a way of leaving room for the world to surprise us—and for people to remain responsible when it does.
